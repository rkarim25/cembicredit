## View
Ethiopia is a defaulted sovereign credit (Fitch RD, S&P SD, Moody's Caa3) nearing completion of external debt restructuring under the G20 Common Framework.
A commercial breakthrough on 29-Jun-26 established an agreement in principle on the sole defaulted USD 1.0bn 2024 notes (XS1151974877).
Restructuring terms provide a 12% principal haircut (to USD 880m), 6.15% coupon due Jul-29, full settlement of USD 99.4m PDI, and a detachable New Money Warrant (or USD 90m cash alternative).
Official bilateral debt relief is anchored by the Jul-25 Memorandum of Understanding with the G20 Official Creditor Committee covering ~$15bn of bilateral debt.
Multilateral backing remains solid through the USD 3.4bn IMF Extended Credit Facility (ECF), with the fifth review completed in Jul-26 bringing cumulative access to ~$2.65bn.
FX liquidity has improved following the Jul-24 Birr float and NBE gold purchases, though inflation rebounded to 13.4% in May-26 due to regional transport and fuel shocks.
Total public debt is moderate at 34%–43% of GDP (USD 33.5bn external as of Mar-26), but fiscal liquidity is squeezed by low tax revenue (~7% of GDP) and security spending.
The primary credit risk is protracted subnational conflict (Amhara and Oromia) disrupting agricultural output and delaying the closing of the Eurobond exchange.
Overall view confidence: Medium.

## Analysis

### 1. Commercial Eurobond restructuring agreement
Ethiopia reached an Agreement in Principle (AIP) with the Ad Hoc Committee of international bondholders on 29-Jun-26 to resolve its sole USD 1.0bn 6.625% Eurobond due 11-Dec-2024 (XS1151974877), which defaulted in December 2023 on a missed USD 33m coupon (MoFED, 29-Jun-26, High). The core terms provide for a 12% nominal principal haircut, swapping the USD 1.0bn claim into USD 880m of new 6.15% fixed-rate notes maturing on 15-Jul-2029 (Ad Hoc Committee, 29-Jun-26, High). The principal amortises in four annual instalments: USD 180m in July 2026, USD 100m in July 2027, USD 300m in July 2028, and USD 300m in July 2029 (MoFED, 29-Jun-26, High). Past-due interest (PDI) of USD 99.4m is to be settled in full in cash upon closing, alongside a 0.5% consent fee (MoFED, 29-Jun-26, High). Bondholders also receive a detachable New Money Warrant giving holders subscription rights to a future USD 1.0bn international debt issuance, with an alternative state option to settle via a USD 90m cash payment (Ad Hoc Committee, 29-Jun-26, High).

### 2. G20 Common Framework and official bilateral debt
Official sector debt relief is governed by the Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) signed in July 2025 with the G20 Official Creditor Committee (OCC), co-chaired by France and China, covering approximately USD 15bn of official bilateral liabilities (Paris Club, 15-Jul-25, High). The agreement established the baseline parameters under the G20 Common Framework's Comparability of Treatment (CoT) requirements (IMF, Jul-26, High). A previous commercial restructuring deal agreed in January 2026 carrying a 15% haircut had faced delays due to OCC scrutiny over comparability metrics (Fitch, Oct-25, Medium). The revised 29-Jun-26 agreement was structured in close consultation with the IMF and OCC secretariat to avoid further comparability challenges, although final formal OCC confirmation on the treatment of the New Money Warrant remains pending (MoFED, 29-Jun-26, Medium).

### 3. IMF programme engagement and macro stabilization
Macroeconomic conditionality and external liquidity are underpinned by the 48-month USD 3.4bn Extended Credit Facility (ECF) approved by the IMF Executive Board on 29-Jul-24 (IMF, 29-Jul-24, High). The Fund completed the fifth review of the programme on 15-Jul-26, approving a disbursement of approximately USD 464m and bringing cumulative disbursements to roughly USD 2.65bn (IMF, 15-Jul-26, High). In response to external disruptions stemming from Middle East conflicts impacting regional fertilizer and fuel shipping, the IMF rephased USD 200m in programme financing to support balance-of-payments needs (IMF, 15-Jul-26, High). World Bank development policy financing and co-financing have provided complementary budget support, ensuring positive net multilateral transfers throughout the restructuring window (World Bank, Jun-26, High).

### 4. FX regime float, reserve accumulation, and inflation
On 29-Jul-24, the National Bank of Ethiopia (NBE) transitioned from a heavily managed crawl to a market-determined floating exchange rate regime, allowing commercial banks to transact foreign currency at market-clearing rates and eliminating import FX surrender rationing (NBE, 29-Jul-24, High). The reform significantly narrowed the parallel market exchange premium from over 100% to single digits (NBE, Jul-26, High). Gross external reserves have rebounded from critical lows (<0.5 months of imports in early 2024), driven by central bank purchases of artisanal gold exceeding USD 2.5bn annualized alongside multilateral inflows (NBE, Jul-26, High). Domestic inflation, which exceeded 30% in 2024, decelerated into single digits in late 2025 before rebounding to 13.4% in May-26 due to transport disruptions and imported energy costs (StatEt, May-26, High). Monetary policy remains restrictive to contain secondary inflation pass-through (NBE, Jul-26, High).

### 5. Fiscal profile and debt sustainability
Total public debt is moderate relative to output, standing in the range of 34% to 43% of GDP in FY26 (IMF, 15-Jul-26, Medium). Total external debt stood at USD 33.5bn as of end-March 2026, dominated by multilateral and bilateral official creditors (MoFED, 31-Mar-26, High). Despite moderate headline debt ratios, debt servicing capacity is severely constrained by an exceptionally low tax revenue base (~7% of GDP), which drives high debt-service-to-revenue ratios and an IMF classification of "in debt distress" pending restructuring completion (IMF, 15-Jul-26, High). Fiscal consolidation efforts face headwinds from spending rigidities, including domestic reconstruction needs and internal security outlays (MoFED, 31-Mar-26, Medium).

### 6. Subnational conflict and political risk
Internal political and security conditions represent the most acute credit risk (UN OCHA, Oct-26, Medium). Armed clashes involving Fano militias in the Amhara region and the Oromo Liberation Army (OLA) in Oromia continue to disrupt transport corridors, agricultural output, and local tax collection (UN OCHA, Oct-26, Medium). While federal authorities retain control over major administrative hubs and arterial highways, localized instability presents ongoing execution risks for fiscal consolidation targets and could complicate the administrative closing of the Eurobond exchange offer (Fitch, Oct-25, Low).
