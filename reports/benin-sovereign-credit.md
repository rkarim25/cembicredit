## Analysis

### Executive transition and policy continuity
Benin achieved a milestone in regional political stability with the peaceful, orderly constitutional transition of power in April–May 2026 (Constitutional Court of Benin, 15-Apr-26, High). President Patrice Talon adhered strictly to the constitutional two-term limit, declining to pursue extension amendments (Presidency of Benin, 10-Jan-26, High). Former Minister of Economy and Finance Romuald Wadagni—the architect of Benin's decade-long fiscal overhaul, debt management strategy, and international capital market access—won the 12-Apr-26 presidential election and assumed office on 24-May-26 (Constitutional Court of Benin, 15-Apr-26, High). This executive succession eliminates political risk discount, ensures continuity of medium-term fiscal frameworks, and preserves orthodox investor-friendly economic governance (S&P, 22-May-26, High).

### Debt profile and external amortisation cliff
External debt vulnerability has dropped sharply following the full redemption of the €500m 5.75% Eurobond (XS1963478018) upon maturity on 26-Mar-26 (Ministry of Economy and Finance, 31-Mar-26, High). The sovereign faces no further Eurobond maturities for six years, with the next bullet maturity falling due in January 2032 (€1.0bn 4.875%, XS2278994418) (Ministry of Economy and Finance, 31-Mar-26, High). The outstanding hard-currency commercial debt perimeter is well-staggered: €1.0bn due 2032, €500m due 2035 (XS2366832496), $750m due 2038 (XS2759982064, issued Jan-24), and the €400m SDG tranche due 2052 (Ministry of Economy and Finance, 31-Mar-26, High). With no near-term market refinancing wall, external debt service consists predominantly of low-cost, long-tenor multilateral and bilateral obligations (IMF, 25-Feb-26, High). Total central government debt stands at ~54% of GDP, rising to ~60.5% when including SOE liabilities expanded under the IMF's perimeter, well within sustainable thresholds (IMF, 25-Feb-26, Medium).

### Fiscal consolidation and WAEMU convergence
Benin remains the fiscal benchmark within the West African Economic and Monetary Union (WAEMU). The overall fiscal deficit narrowed from 3.1% of GDP in 2024 to ~2.8%–2.9% in 2025, bringing the budget back within the WAEMU 3.0% deficit ceiling ahead of schedule (Ministry of Economy and Finance / IMF, 25-Feb-26, High). Tax revenue mobilisation improved to ~14.5% of GDP, driven by digitalization of customs declarations at the Port of Cotonou and property tax cadastral reforms (IMF, 25-Feb-26, High). Interest expense to revenue is contained at ~14%, well below the 20% stress boundary observed in regional peers (Moody's, 14-Aug-26, High). The primary balance has converged to near debt-stabilizing levels (estimated pb* ≈ -0.8% of GDP vs actual primary deficit of -0.6%), placing public debt on a gradual downward trajectory toward 50% of GDP by 2028 (IMF, 25-Feb-26, Medium).

### Macroeconomic growth and structural diversification
Real GDP growth expanded by 7.5% in 2025 and is projected to expand by ~6.5% in 2026, ranking Benin among the fastest-growing economies in Africa (INSAE / IMF, 25-Feb-26, High). Expansion is anchored by major investments in the Port of Cotonou modernization, logistics corridor upgrades, and rapid development of the Glo-Djigbé Industrial Zone (GDIZ), which has scaled local processing of cotton, cashew nuts, and textiles (Ministry of Economy and Finance, 31-Mar-26, High). Agricultural productivity gains and public infrastructure execution under the Government Action Plan (PAG II) continue to bolster domestic value addition and reduce susceptibility to raw commodity price shocks (S&P, 22-May-26, Medium).

### External position, FX regime, and pipeline transit
Benin operates under the WAEMU framework with the CFA franc pegged to the euro at 655.957, guaranteed by the French Treasury and managed by the BCEAO (BCEAO, 15-Aug-26, High). This institutional anchor eliminates sovereign exchange-rate convertibility risk and anchors headline inflation at ~1.0%–1.5% y/y (BCEAO / INSAE, 15-Aug-26, High). The current account deficit widened moderately to ~5.5% of GDP in 2025, driven by capital goods imports for industrial zones, but remains comfortably financed by concessional multilateral inflows, project grants, and sticky foreign direct investment (IMF, 25-Feb-26, Medium). The commissioning of the 2,000 km Niger–Benin crude export pipeline terminating at Sèmè-Kpodji offers long-term port transit fee potential, though initial volumes were delayed by periodic border tensions with Niger's military regime (Ministry of Economy and Finance, 31-Mar-26, Medium).

### Regional security and northern border risks
The primary downside risk to the credit profile stems from regional security spillovers. Jihadist insurgent groups operating in eastern Burkina Faso and south-western Niger have carried out intermittent attacks in Benin's northern Atakora and Alibori departments (Fitch, 20-Jun-26, High). While the government launched "Operation Mirador" and reinforced border deployments, security-related expenditures have increased to ~1.5% of GDP (Fitch, 20-Jun-26, Medium). Prolonged regional border friction or supply chain disruptions along the transit corridor to Niamey represent the main latent vulnerability for port traffic and transit revenue (Moody's, 14-Aug-26, Medium).
