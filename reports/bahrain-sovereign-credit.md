## View
- Bahrain operates as a fragile single-B sovereign (Fitch B Stable; S&P B Stable; Moody's B2 Negative) whose solvency is entirely anchored by an implicit GCC geopolitical backstop (Rating Agencies, Apr-26, High).
- Public debt sustainability is severely stretched, with gross government debt reaching 134% of GDP in 2024 and remaining above 133% across 2025–2026 (IMF Article IV, Jan-26, High).
- Fiscal deficits remain entrenched near double digits (11.0% of GDP in 2024, ~10.8% in 2025) as the fiscal breakeven oil price exceeds $90/bbl against lower market crude prices (IMF, Jan-26, High).
- Domestic monetary financing is high: the Central Bank of Bahrain overdraft facility exceeded 25% of GDP in 2024 to fund fiscal gaps, though contracting ~8% by late 2025 (IMF, Jan-26, High).
- External liquidity is vulnerable, with gross FX reserves providing barely two months of prospective non-hydrocarbon import cover, leaving the 0.376 BHD/USD peg reliant on GCC deposits (CBB / IMF, Jan-26, High).
- Commercial market access remains resilient: Bahrain successfully placed $1.0bn 10-year Eurobonds at 7.125% in June 2026 (XS3381699902) backed by robust GCC regional orderbooks (Ministry of Finance, Jun-26, High).
- Secondary USD Eurobond spreads trade near ~329 bps G-spread (~8.33% average yield), offering attractive carry that strictly prices GCC underwriting rather than standalone credit fundamentals (Cbonds / Secondary Quotes, Sep-26, Medium).
- Primary downside risk is a sharp drop in oil prices below $70/bbl coinciding with Gulf geopolitical friction or delays in bilateral GCC emergency liquidity disbursements (Market Sources, Sep-26, Medium).
- Transparency remains impaired after the authorities withheld consent to publish the full 2025 IMF Article IV Staff Report and Debt Sustainability Analysis (IMF, Jan-26, High).
- Overall confidence: High on official debt, deficit, and rating actions; Low on secondary pricing and GCC facility terms until verified via [BBG] and [MANAGEMENT].

## Analysis

### Sovereign rating downgrades across all agencies into the single-B tier (B / B2 / B)
- Sovereign credit ratings have fully transitioned into single-B junk status across all three major international rating agencies between late 2025 and early 2026, removing any remaining expectation of investment-grade convergence (Rating Agencies, Apr-26, High).
- Fitch Ratings downgraded Bahrain from B+ to B with a Stable outlook on 23-Feb-2026, citing government debt exceeding 130% of GDP, persistent fiscal deficits, and heavy reliance on domestic banking liquidity (Fitch Ratings, 23-Feb-26, High).
- S&P Global Ratings downgraded Bahrain to B with a Stable outlook on 21-Nov-2025 (affirmed May-2026), highlighting structural fiscal rigidities, sluggish progress under the Fiscal Balance Program, and elevated external debt service burdens (S&P Global Ratings, 21-Nov-25, High).
- Moody's Ratings revised the outlook on Bahrain's B2 sovereign issuer rating to Negative from Stable on 17-Apr-2026, reflecting escalating government debt burdens and heightened refinancing sensitivity amid tight international liquidity conditions (Moody's Ratings, 17-Apr-26, High).
- The synchronized downgrade trajectory reflects rating agency consensus that Bahrain's standalone creditworthiness is severely impaired, with current ratings heavily reliant on an imputed multi-notch uplift for GCC sovereign support (Rating Agencies, Apr-26, High).

### Public debt overhang (134% of GDP) and elevated fiscal breakeven oil price (~$90–$100/bbl)
- Public debt sustainability has deteriorated to critical levels: the IMF 2025 Article IV consultation (concluded 23-Jan-2026) confirmed that gross government debt reached 134% of GDP in 2024 and is projected to remain elevated above 133% of GDP across 2025–2026 (IMF Article IV, 23-Jan-26, High).
- The central government headline fiscal deficit deteriorated to 11.0% of GDP in 2024 and remained near 10.8%–11.0% in 2025, falling drastically short of the authorities' Fiscal Balance Program (FBP) commitment to achieve a balanced budget by 2024 (IMF Article IV, 23-Jan-26, High).
- Bahrain's fiscal breakeven oil price remains exceedingly high at ~$90–$100 per barrel of Brent, leaving the budget highly exposed to crude price fluctuations despite the introduction of a 10% VAT in 2022 (Rating Agencies / IMF, Jan-26, High).
- Spending rigidities are acute: government wage bills, social subsidies, and escalating debt interest expenditure consume over 85% of total government revenues, preventing meaningful discretionary fiscal consolidation without disruptive social consequences (Ministry of Finance, Dec-25, High).
- Debt interest service alone absorbs ~25%–30% of total fiscal revenues, representing one of the highest interest-to-revenue burdens among CEEMEA sovereigns and severely crowding out capital development expenditures (Rating Agencies / IMF, Jan-26, High).

### Central Bank of Bahrain balance-sheet stress, domestic overdraft facility, and FX reserve adequacy
- Domestic deficit financing has increasingly relied on direct monetary expansion: the IMF reported that the government overdraft facility at the Central Bank of Bahrain (CBB) expanded beyond 25% of GDP in 2024 to plug budgetary cash shortfalls (IMF Article IV, 23-Jan-26, High).
- Although the authorities contracted the CBB overdraft by approximately 8% by October 2025 through domestic Treasury bill issuance, the central bank's balance sheet remains heavily encumbered by sovereign exposure (IMF Article IV, 23-Jan-26, High).
- Gross foreign exchange reserves at the CBB remain precariously thin, providing barely two months of prospective non-hydrocarbon import cover (~$4.5bn–$5.0bn gross), falling well below the standard IMF three-month adequacy benchmark (Central Bank of Bahrain / IMF, Jan-26, High).
- Furthermore, a substantial portion of CBB headline foreign reserves is constituted by bilateral deposits placed by regional central banks, implying that net usable reserves (excluding foreign central bank liabilities) are significantly lower or negative (Rating Agencies, Apr-26, Medium).
- The long-standing currency peg of 0.3760 Bahraini Dinars (BHD) per US Dollar (in place since 1980) remains vulnerable to capital outflows, surviving primarily through implicit guarantees of GCC liquidity injections during periods of balance of payments stress (Central Bank of Bahrain, Sep-26, High).

### Implicit GCC geopolitical backstop and post-2018 financial support framework
- Bahrain's credit survival is structurally dependent on the political and financial commitment of its wealthy Gulf neighbours: Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Kuwait (Rating Agencies, Apr-26, High).
- In 2018, the GCC troika committed a $10.0bn zero-interest financial aid package linked to the Fiscal Balance Program; this facility disbursed its final tranches between 2023 and 2024 to support debt repayments and reserve stability (Ministry of Finance / IMF, Jan-26, High).
- Following the completion of the original $10bn envelope, the Gulf allies have not formally announced a new multi-year institutionalized funding facility, though bilateral support continues through project financing (Saudi-Bahraini Coordination Council) and central bank deposit rollovers (Regional Press, Jun-26, Medium).
- The geopolitical imperative for Saudi Arabia and the UAE to maintain political and economic stability in Bahrain—preventing Iranian influence across the King Fahd Causeway—ensures that financial support will be provided in a liquidity emergency, capping ultimate default risk (Strategic Analysis, Sep-26, High).
- However, the absence of an open, institutionalized replacement framework creates periodic refinancing anxiety whenever global risk aversion spikes or regional oil revenues contract (Market Sources, Sep-26, Medium).

### Commercial Eurobond market access, curve valuation, and relative value
- Despite single-B ratings and severe debt metrics, Bahrain retains consistent commercial market access, supported by dedicated regional GCC bank liquidity and Islamic asset managers (Market Sources, Jun-26, High).
- In June 2026, the Ministry of Finance successfully issued $1.0bn of 10-year USD conventional Eurobonds due 10-Jun-2036 (ISIN XS3381699902) at a yield of 7.125%, attracting an orderbook in excess of $3.2bn (Ministry of Finance, 10-Jun-26, High).
- The broader Bahrain sovereign USD curve trades at an average yield of ~8.33% with a G-spread of ~329 bps, reflecting a significant risk premium over GCC investment-grade peers (Oman at ~75 bps, Saudi Arabia at ~80 bps) but inside distressed CEEMEA peers like Egypt (~650 bps) or Kenya (~700 bps) (Cbonds Bahrain Sovereign USD Index, 29-Sep-26, Medium).
- Indicative secondary curve benchmarks reflect: BHRAIN 7.000% Jan-28 at ~6.85% yield; BHRAIN 5.625% May-34 at ~7.80% yield; BHRAIN 7.125% Jun-36 at ~8.15% yield; BHRAIN 7.500% Sep-47 at ~8.75% yield (Secondary Quotes, Sep-26, Medium).
- From a relative value perspective, holding Bahrain debt is fundamentally an asymmetric bet on GCC underwriting: investors capture ~8.3% yield and ~330 bps spread while taking sovereign risk that is economically distressed but politically backstopped (Fund Manager View, Sep-26, Medium).

### Contingent liabilities across SOEs (Bapco Energies, Mumtalakat, Alba) and data transparency
- Bahrain carries substantial off-balance-sheet contingent liabilities across key state-owned enterprises, led by Bapco Energies (formerly Nogaholding), Aluminium Bahrain (Alba), and sovereign investment arm Mumtalakat (Rating Agencies, Dec-25, High).
- Bapco Energies' multi-billion dollar Bapco Modernization Program (BMP) to expand the Sitra refinery capacity to 400,000 bpd involves extensive syndicated commercial and export credit debt that carries implicit sovereign repayment responsibility (Bapco Energies, Dec-25, High).
- Fiscal and debt transparency suffered a notable setback in early 2026: the IMF officially noted that the Bahraini authorities did not consent to the publication of the full 2025 Article IV Staff Report or accompanying Debt Sustainability Analysis (DSA) tables (IMF Press Release, 23-Jan-26, High).
- Non-publication of the IMF staff report conceals official baseline debt sustainability paths, detailed financing gap projections, and contingent liability stress tests from public scrutiny, increasing reliance on independent model estimates (IMF, 23-Jan-26, High).
