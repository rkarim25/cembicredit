## View
- Jordan is an anchored double-B sovereign (S&P BB- Stable; Fitch BB- Stable; Moody's Ba3 Stable) combining high public debt with exceptional geopolitical and multilateral underwriting (Rating Agencies, Aug-26, High).
- Public debt is elevated but peaking: central government debt reached 83.6% of GDP in 2025 (up from 82.1% in 2024), targeted to decline toward ~82.5% in 2026 and 80.0% by 2028 under the IMF EFF framework (IMF / MoF, Jun-26, High).
- External liquidity buffers are exceptionally strong: CBJ gross FX reserves reached a record $28.5bn in September 2026 (+11.7% YTD), providing 9.2 months of import cover and solidly defending the 0.709 JOD/USD peg (Central Bank of Jordan, Sep-26, High).
- Multilateral and official financing anchors the macro framework: the 4-year $1.3bn IMF Extended Fund Facility (EFF) completed its Fifth Review in June 2026 alongside RSF climate funding without policy slippage (IMF, Jun-26, High).
- Geopolitical underwriting from the US and GCC is structural: the 7-year US MoU provides $1.45bn annually through FY2029 ($10.15bn total), funding over half of gross external financing requirements (US State Dept / MoP, Sep-22, High).
- Fiscal consolidation is sustained: primary fiscal deficit contained to 2.8% of GDP in 2024 (overall deficit 5.2% of GDP), though structural losses at state utilities NEPCO and WAJ remain persistent drags (IMF / MoF, Jun-26, High).
- Commercial market access is open on tight pricing: issued $700m 7-year Eurobonds at 5.75% in Nov-25 (XS3218674136), with secondary spreads trading tight at ~188 bps T-spread (~7.48% YTM) (MoF / Cbonds, Sep-26, High).
- Primary risk is Middle East regional war escalation disrupting tourism receipts (~12% of GDP), trade transit via Aqaba port, and domestic energy transit pipelines (Market Sources, Sep-26, Medium).
- Sovereign macro-fiscal openpyxl model must be compiled from primary official sources (MoF, CBJ, DoS, and IMF) to audit NEPCO/WAJ contingent debt paths (Data Gap, Oct-26, High).
- Overall confidence: High on official debt, FX reserves, IMF EFF compliance, and bilateral aid; Medium on secondary pricing and war spillover duration until verified via [BBG].

## Analysis

### Double-B rating convergence and institutional reform credibility (BB- / BB- / Ba3)
- Sovereign credit ratings are solidly anchored in the double-B category across all three major agencies, reflecting structural reform progress under IMF auspices that differentiates Jordan from regional single-B distress (Rating Agencies, Aug-26, High).
- S&P Global Ratings affirmed Jordan at BB- with a Stable outlook on 28-Aug-2026, maintaining the upgrade achieved in September 2024 from B+ on the back of resilient macroeconomic management and strong donor backing (S&P Global Ratings, 28-Aug-26, High).
- Fitch Ratings affirmed Jordan at BB- with a Stable outlook in May 2026, citing prudent fiscal governance, fiscal deficit compression, and demonstrated external debt service track record (Fitch Ratings, May-26, High).
- Moody's Ratings confirmed Jordan at Ba3 with a Stable outlook in April 2026, following its upgrade from B1 in May 2024, highlighting domestic revenue mobilization and institutional debt management capacity (Moody's Ratings, Apr-26, High).
- Rating agency stability underscores that Jordan's high debt load is perceived as sustainable due to deeply institutionalized official financing pipelines and exceptionally low reliance on short-term external commercial debt (Rating Agencies, Aug-26, High).

### Elevated public debt stock (83.6% of GDP) anchored by IMF consolidation path toward 80% by 2028
- Central government debt reached 83.6% of GDP in 2025 (up from 82.1% in 2024), reflecting regional economic headwinds and energy subsidy adjustments, but is projected to moderate toward ~82.5% in 2026 (IMF / Ministry of Finance, Jun-26, High).
- Under the IMF Extended Fund Facility (EFF), the authorities are legally and policy-committed to reducing public debt to 80.0% of GDP by 2028 through targeted primary surplus generation (IMF Country Report, Jun-26, High).
- The central government primary deficit was contained to 2.8% of GDP in 2024, while the headline fiscal deficit recorded 5.2% of GDP, outperforming peers through broadened income tax coverage and electronic billing enforcement (Ministry of Finance, Dec-25, High).
- Spending rigidities are substantial: public sector wages, military expenditures, pensions, and debt interest consume over 70% of total budgetary expenditures, constraining discretionary capital development spending (Ministry of Finance, Dec-25, High).
- Interest expenditure absorbs approximately 15%–17% of central government revenues, a manageable burden compared to single-B peers like Egypt (>50%) or Nigeria (>35%), supported by concessional multilateral borrowing rates (IMF, Jun-26, High).

### External liquidity strength, record FX reserves ($28.5bn / 9.2 months import cover), and peg credibility
- Central Bank of Jordan (CBJ) gross foreign exchange reserves reached an all-time record of $28.5bn at end-September 2026, representing an 11.7% YTD increase ($3.0bn net build) from end-2025 (Central Bank of Jordan, Sep-26, High).
- Reserve adequacy is among the strongest in CEEMEA: gross FX reserves provide 9.2 months of prospective goods and services imports, well exceeding the IMF 3-month benchmark and the Greenspan-Guidotti short-term debt cover rule (Central Bank of Jordan, Sep-26, High).
- The long-standing currency peg of 0.7090 Jordanian Dinars (JOD) per US Dollar (1 JOD = 1.4104 USD, maintained since 1995) remains unquestioned, supported by CBJ policy rate alignment with the US Federal Reserve (currently CBJ discount rate at 6.00%) (Central Bank of Jordan, Sep-26, High).
- Current account deficits remain financed by official grants, multilateral disbursements, resilient remittances from Jordanian expatriates in the GCC (~$3.5bn–$4.0bn annually), and recovering travel receipts outside immediate border conflict zones (Central Bank of Jordan, Sep-26, High).

### IMF Extended Fund Facility ($1.3bn EFF) and multilateral structural anchor
- Jordan is operating under a 4-year IMF Extended Fund Facility (EFF) approved in January 2024 for SDR 926.37m (~$1.3bn), which replaced its previous Extended Arrangement and reinforced policy continuity (IMF Press Release, Jan-24, High).
- In June 2026, the IMF Executive Board successfully completed the Fifth Review under the EFF, approving an immediate disbursement of SDR 97.784m (~$130m), alongside the Second Review under the Resilience and Sustainability Facility (RSF) releasing SDR 39.588m (~$52m) for climate-resilient water infrastructure (IMF Press Release, Jun-26, High).
- Jordan's performance under IMF conditionality is exceptionally consistent across multiple program cycles, avoiding the review delays, policy reversals, and financing freezes experienced by regional peers (IMF Country Report, Jun-26, High).
- Concessional official co-financing from the World Bank, European Investment Bank (EIB), and European Bank for Reconstruction and Development (EBRD) remains closely linked to IMF program compliance, unlocking over $1.0bn in annual subsidized development loans (Ministry of Planning, Jun-26, High).

### US Bilateral Memorandum of Understanding ($1.45bn/yr) and GCC strategic backing
- Jordan's sovereign solvency is heavily underwritten by bilateral geopolitical support, led by the United States under a 7-year Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) covering FY2023–FY2029 (US Department of State, Sep-22, High).
- The MoU provides $1.45bn annually in total bilateral assistance ($10.15bn cumulative), including a minimum of $610m in direct Economic Support Fund (ESF) budget cash transfers and $400m in Foreign Military Financing (FMF) (US Department of State / MoP, Sep-22, High).
- Bilateral US assistance covers more than 50% of the Jordanian government's gross external financing requirement, providing a non-debt financing backstop that immunizes the sovereign from commercial capital market volatility (Ministry of Planning, Dec-25, High).
- Supplementary financial support from GCC allies (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, Kuwait) provides multi-hundred-million-dollar project grants, sovereign wealth fund equity co-investments, and Central Bank deposit rollovers, cementing regional interest in Jordanian political and economic stability (Regional Official Statements, Jun-26, Medium).

### Commercial Eurobond market access, curve valuation, and relative value
- International commercial market access is firmly established: in November 2025, Jordan issued a $700m 7-year Eurobond at 5.75% due 12-Nov-2032 (ISIN XS3218674136), alongside a cash tender offer to retire maturing 2026 debt (Ministry of Finance, Nov-25, High).
- Secondary spreads across Jordan's USD curve trade tight relative to the broader high-yield composite: the Cbonds Jordan Sovereign USD Index reports a blended YTM of ~7.48% and a T-spread of approximately 188 bps over US Treasuries (Cbonds Jordan Sovereign USD Index, 29-Sep-26, Medium).
- Indicative secondary curve benchmarks trade in a disciplined band: JOR 7.75% Jan-28 (XS2490731721) at ~187 bps Z-spread; JOR 5.85% Jul-30 (XS2199272662) at ~192 bps Z-spread; JOR 5.75% Nov-32 (XS3218674136) at ~195 bps Z-spread; JOR 7.375% Oct-47 (XS1577950311) at ~215 bps Z-spread (Secondary Quotes, Sep-26, Medium).
- On a relative value basis, Jordan trades ~140 bps inside single-B GCC neighbor Bahrain (~330 bps spread, 8.33% YTM) and ~450 bps inside Egypt (~650 bps spread), offering an attractive high-quality defensive anchor for EM credit portfolios seeking double-B carry insulated from balance-of-payments crises (Market Analysis, Sep-26, Medium).

### State-owned enterprise contingent liabilities (NEPCO, WAJ) and regional conflict transmission
- The primary domestic fiscal vulnerability stems from structural quasi-fiscal deficits at key state-owned enterprises: the National Electric Power Company (NEPCO) and the Water Authority of Jordan (WAJ) (IMF Country Report, Jun-26, High).
- NEPCO suffers from long-term power purchase agreements (PPAs) that created substantial generation overcapacity, while WAJ incurs heavy operational losses from non-revenue water (theft and distribution leakage) and high pumping energy costs (IMF / World Bank, Jun-26, High).
- Operational losses at NEPCO and WAJ contribute approximately 1.5%–2.0% of GDP annually to government financing requirements, requiring ongoing budget transfers that delay faster public debt reduction (IMF, Jun-26, High).
- External geopolitical transmission channels represent the foremost tail risk: direct escalation of Middle East hostilities disrupts transit trade through Aqaba port, threatens Israeli gas transit pipelines feeding domestic power plants, and depresses regional tourism revenues (~12% of GDP) (Market Intelligence, Sep-26, Medium).
