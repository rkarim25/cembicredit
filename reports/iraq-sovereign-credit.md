Extreme hydrocarbon monoculture leaves creditworthiness tied to crude prices, with oil generating over 90% of fiscal revenue and 95% of merchandise exports.
Hard-currency commercial default risk is idiosyncratic and uniquely light: only one commercial Eurobond remains, the amortising USD 5.80% 2028 ($506.25m outstanding).

## Analysis

### Sovereign credit profile and rating actions
Iraq’s sovereign ratings occupy deep speculative grade territory, reflecting severe institutional fragility, oil price vulnerability, and regional conflict exposure (S&P Global Ratings, 12-Jun-26, High). S&P affirmed its B- rating on 12-Jun-26 but maintained a Negative outlook, citing pressure on fiscal balances and foreign exchange reserves from regional geopolitical instability and OPEC+ production limits (S&P Global Ratings, 12-Jun-26, High). Moody's rates Iraq at Caa1 with a Negative outlook (affirmed 21-Apr-26), highlighting that high recurrent government expenditure has eroded fiscal buffers and elevated external vulnerability (Moody's Ratings, 21-Apr-26, High). Fitch maintains a B- rating with a Stable outlook (affirmed 24-Nov-25), balancing the sovereign's substantial foreign reserve buffer against structural political and security deficits (Fitch Ratings, 24-Nov-25, High).

### Capital structure and external debt profile
Iraq’s commercial external debt structure is uniquely light compared to peer frontier sovereigns (Luxembourg Stock Exchange, 08-Oct-26, High). The sovereign has only a single commercial Eurobond outstanding: the amortising USD 5.80% notes due 15-Jan-2028 (ISIN XS0240295575), originally issued in 2006 for $2.7bn following the Paris Club debt restructuring (Bondblox, 08-Oct-26, High). The remaining principal balance is approximately $506.25m, following successive semi-annual sinking fund amortisations (Bondblox, 08-Oct-26, High). Iraq's previous standalone commercial bond, the USD 6.75% 2023 Eurobond (ISIN XS1662407862), was fully repaid at its March 2023 maturity without incident (Ministry of Finance, 31-Dec-23, High). Total external debt stands at approximately $54.1bn (2025), but is overwhelmingly composed of bilateral Paris Club restructuring agreements, non-Paris Club legacy claims, and project loans from multilateral development lenders such as the World Bank and JICA rather than marketable bonds (Ministry of Finance Public Debt Directorate, 31-Jul-26, Medium).

### Fiscal position and breakeven oil dynamics
Iraq suffers from acute fiscal rigidity generated by an unhedged public sector wage bill that has expanded to cover more than 4 million civil servants and state employees (IMF Article IV, 20-May-25, High). Recurrent compensation and social transfers consume more than 75% of total budget expenditure, driving the fiscal breakeven oil price above $90 per barrel (IMF Article IV, 20-May-25, High). With crude export revenues constrained by OPEC+ quota allocations (~3.9–4.0 mbpd) and lower average realized oil prices, the budget deficit reached 4.2% of GDP in 2024 and is projected to widen across 2025–2026 (IMF Article IV, 20-May-25, High). The central government has financed this gap primarily through domestic borrowing: domestic public debt expanded from IQD 90.5trn ($69.2bn) at end-2025 to IQD 109.5trn ($83.8bn) at end-July 2026, creating severe encumbrance across state-owned commercial banks Rafidain and Rasheed (Central Bank of Iraq, 31-Jul-26, High). Total public debt to GDP is projected to rise to ~57% by year-end 2026 from 53.9% in 2025 (IMF / Ministry of Finance, 31-Jul-26, Medium).

### Monetary policy, exchange rate devaluation, and CBI reserves
To relieve acute dinar cash shortages required to fund state payrolls, the Central Bank of Iraq (CBI) enacted a sharp official currency devaluation on 07-Oct-26 (Central Bank of Iraq, 07-Oct-26, High). The official selling price of the dollar was adjusted by 15.2% from 1,320 IQD/USD to 1,520 IQD/USD (with the CBI purchasing dollars from the Ministry of Finance at 1,500 IQD/USD and selling to licensed banks at 1,510 IQD/USD) (Central Bank of Iraq, 07-Oct-26, High). Following the devaluation, the parallel market exchange rate in Baghdad and Erbil depreciated past 1,700 IQD/USD, generating an informal spread above 12% (Local Market Reports, 07-Oct-26, Medium). Despite defending the currency, CBI gross international reserves declined by 17% over the first seven months of 2026, falling from $97.4bn at end-2025 to $80.6bn at end-July 2026 (Central Bank of Iraq, 31-Jul-26, High). However, at $80.6bn, reserves still provide 9.6 months of import cover and exceed the remaining commercial Eurobond principal ($506.25m) by more than 150 times, providing a robust near-term liquidity backstop for hard-currency bondholders (Central Bank of Iraq / IMF, 31-Jul-26, High).

### Geopolitical vulnerabilities and Strait of Hormuz transmission
Iraq's macroeconomic stability is acutely exposed to regional military escalation across the Persian Gulf (S&P Global Ratings, 12-Jun-26, High). Following the protracted shutdown of the northern Kirkuk-Ceyhan pipeline (ITP) through Turkey, which previously evacuated ~450 kbpd, Iraq is almost entirely dependent on southern marine terminals in Basra (SOMO Monthly Export Bulletins, 31-Aug-26, High). Approximately 3.4 mbpd of crude exports must transit maritime choke points in the Persian Gulf and the Strait of Hormuz (SOMO, 31-Aug-26, High). Any military disruption or insurance suspension in the Persian Gulf would instantly cut off over 85% of government fiscal receipts and foreign currency inflows, triggering immediate balance-of-payments distress regardless of reserve scale (S&P Global Ratings, 12-Jun-26, High). Real GDP is projected to contract by 6.8% in 2026 under oil production ceilings and transit bottlenecks (IMF Projections, 20-May-25, Medium).

### Multilateral relations and IMF engagement
Iraq currently has no active financial lending arrangement with the International Monetary Fund (IMF Countries Portal, 08-Oct-26, High). The last formal Stand-By Arrangement (SBA) expired in 2018 (IMF Country Records, 08-Oct-26, High). Engagement is conducted through standard Article IV annual surveillance and Post-Financing Assessment (PFA) reviews, meaning there is no programmatic policy conditionality or multilateral emergency financing facility currently in place to anchor structural fiscal reforms (IMF Country Records, 08-Oct-26, High).
