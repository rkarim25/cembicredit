# Official sector: IMF, Paris Club, Common Framework, China

How the official sector shapes EM sovereign outcomes. Facts here are structural; case-specific facts go to the country pages.

## IMF
- Lending instruments and what they signal: see glossary. Size in percent of quota; above 435 percent cumulative triggers exceptional access criteria (debt sustainable with high probability, market access prospects, programme success, political assurances).
- Programme mechanics that matter for bonds: prior actions before board approval, quantitative performance criteria (reserves floors, primary balance, no new arrears), structural benchmarks, reviews (quarterly or semi-annual), waivers and modifications, financing assurances from other creditors.
- Lending into arrears: the Fund can disburse while the sovereign is in default to private creditors if negotiations are in good faith (LIA) and to official creditors under LIOA conditions. This lets a programme anchor a restructuring.
- DSA: the LIC-DSF uses PV of external debt to GDP and exports, debt service to exports and revenue, with thresholds by debt-carrying capacity; the MAC-DSA uses gross financing needs and debt to GDP fan charts. The DSA's targets are the restructuring envelope.
- RSF: climate-linked long-term money that often tops up a programme for countries like Kenya, Côte d'Ivoire, Senegal, Morocco.

## Paris Club
- Classic, Houston, Naples, Cologne terms by income level; cut-off dates; comparability of treatment clause in every agreed minute.
- Non-Paris Club creditors (China, India, Gulf states, Turkey) are not bound; this is why the Common Framework exists.

## G20 Common Framework (2020)
- For DSSI-eligible (low-income) countries. Creditor committee co-chaired by Paris Club and a non-Paris Club creditor, usually China. Cases: Chad (2022), Zambia (2023 MoU), Ghana (2024 MoU), Ethiopia (2024–25). Timelines ran from one to three years; the Global Sovereign Debt Roundtable has tried to shorten them.
- Middle-income countries (Sri Lanka, Suriname) used ad hoc official creditor committees outside the Framework with similar mechanics.

## China as a creditor
- Lenders: China Exim (concessional and preferential buyer's credits), China Development Bank (commercial), commercial banks, and swap lines from the PBoC that count in reserves but are not freely usable.
- Behaviour: preference for maturity extension and interest reduction over face haircuts; insistence that multilaterals share the burden; slow committee participation. Collateralised and resource-backed loans (Angola, Ecuador historically) complicate the perimeter.
- Swap-line drawings inflate gross reserves; net them when assessing adequacy (Argentina, Pakistan, Egypt, Nigeria at various points).

## Gulf anchors
- Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar and Kuwait provide deposits at central banks, oil facilities and investment pledges (Egypt, Pakistan, Turkey, Jordan, Bahrain, Oman historically). Terms shifted after 2022 toward investment-for-support rather than grants; conditionality is political and economic. Treat deposits as rollover-dependent liabilities, not reserves, unless the maturity is long.

## Multilateral development banks
- World Bank (IDA and IBRD), AfDB, EBRD, AIIB, Afreximbank (a contested preferred-creditor claim in Ghana and Zambia cases). Preferred creditor status in practice means their debt is excluded from restructurings; the larger their share, the smaller the pie for bondholders.
